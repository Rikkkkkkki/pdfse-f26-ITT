# Problem Statement

## The statement

First-time software builders and junior students with personal project ideas, like Giorgi in Kutaisi, struggle to transition from passive learning to writing their own code (01, 05, 06). The breakdown occurs in the evening when they open their laptop to start their project and immediately encounter conflicting tutorials or tech stack opinions (01, 05, 06). This costs them between two and five weeks of delay, leaving them with abandoned project folders and empty repositories (01, 05, 06). Today, they cope by building Notion tables of unwatched courses, comparing stacks across spreadsheets, and restarting with different engines whenever someone suggests a new framework (01, 05, 06). These workarounds fail because organizing resources creates an illusion of productivity without producing working software, leaving learners trapped thinking "every time I watch a new video I think I'm doing it wrong" (05) and acknowledging "I spent more time making a Notion page of courses than learning anything" (01).

## What the evidence ruled out

- **Lack of tutorials or information:** We originally predicted that beginners struggle because they cannot find what to learn. Logs 01, 05, and 06 demonstrated the opposite: educational resources are overwhelmingly abundant, and excess conflicting advice directly fuels paralysis.
- **Universal developer struggle:** Log 04 ruled out that all student developers share this problem; students with 2+ years of shipping experience dive into coding immediately and debug as they go, without starting paralysis.

## What is still open

- Whether providing a single opinionated starter stack and a day-one milestone breaks the analysis paralysis immediately, or if the fear of missing out still leads learners to second-guess and hoard alternative tutorials.

## Pattern behind it

- **Pattern:** Beginners mistake organizing learning resources for progress because they fear committing to the wrong stack or tutorial path.
- **Root cause:** Beginners use resource curation and stack comparisons as a psychological substitute for progress to shield themselves from the fear of committing to the wrong technology.
